Methodology

How This Works

For each person, we take their realized record (W-L-P) and the American odds of every pick, and convert those odds into implied win probabilities (assuming no house edge).

We then run a Monte Carlo simulation — 1,000,000 trials per person — to build the distribution of possible win counts (W) given those implied probabilities. From that distribution we compute a p-value: P(W ≥ w), where w is the person's actual win count.

A low p-value means a record this good would be rare by chance alone — a sign of skill. A high p-value means the implied odds predicted a much better record than they actually posted.

Each entry in the Weekly Snapshots and charts above is cumulative, not just that week in isolation: a given week's p-value is computed from every pick made from the start of the season (or, on the All-Time tab, from the start of tracking) through that week, not only the picks made during that specific week.

Limitations: Posted American odds include the sportsbook's vig, which isn't removed here — so implied probabilities run slightly higher than each bet's true fair probability. Even a bettor with zero real edge should expect a p-value somewhat above 0.5 on average; treat p-values as relative rankings within the group for a given week, not as absolute proof of being below average. Pushes count as non-wins (grouped with losses) rather than being dropped from the simulation, so the simulated sample size always matches the number of real picks.
